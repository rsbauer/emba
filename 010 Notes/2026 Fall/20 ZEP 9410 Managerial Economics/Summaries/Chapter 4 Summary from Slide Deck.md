Here is a summary of Chapter 4: **The Theory of Individual Behavior** from the slide deck:

### **1. Consumer Preferences and Indifference Curves**

- Consumer behavior is driven by opportunities (what can be afforded) and preferences (what gives satisfaction).
    
- **Four properties of preferences:**
    
    - **Completeness:** A consumer can rank any two bundles ($A > B$, $B > A$, or $A \sim B$).
        
    - **More is better:** Bundles with strictly more of every good (and no less of others) are preferred.
        
    - **Diminishing marginal rate of substitution:** As a consumer gets more of good $X$, they are willing to give up less of good $Y$ to get another unit of $X$.
        
    - **Transitivity:** Preferences are consistent ($A > B$ and $B > C$ implies $A > C$).
        
- **Indifference curves** map out all combinations of two goods that yield the exact same level of satisfaction.
    

### **2. The Budget Constraint and Market Opportunities**

- The **budget set** defines all affordable combinations of goods $X$ and $Y$ where $P_X X + P_Y Y \le M$.
    
- The **budget line** represents bundles that completely exhaust consumer income ($P_X X + P_Y Y = M$).
    
- Changes in income shift the budget line parallelly (expanding or shrinking opportunities), while changes in price pivot the budget line by altering its intercept.
    

### **3. Consumer Equilibrium**

- **Consumer equilibrium** occurs at the point on the budget line where the consumer maximizes satisfaction.
    
- Mathematically, this happens where the marginal rate of substitution ($MRS$) equals the market rate of substitution ($P_X / P_Y$).
    
- Price and income changes shift this equilibrium point, allowing economists to classify goods as substitutes, complements, normal goods, or inferior goods.
    

### **4. Substitution and Income Effects**

- When a good's price changes, the total impact on consumption can be broken down into two distinct parts:
    
    - **Substitution effect:** Movement along a single indifference curve driven purely by changes in relative prices, holding real income constant.
        
    - **Income effect:** Movement from one indifference curve to another driven by the change in purchasing power (real income) resulting from the price change.
        

### **5. Applications and Deriving Demand Curves**

- **Indifference curve analysis applies to practical choices:**
    
    - Gift certificates and in-kind vs. cash gifts.
        
    - **Labor-leisure choice framework:** Analyzes how workers allocate time between leisure and work based on wages and non-labor income.
        
- **Demand Derivation:** Tracing out consumer equilibrium points as prices change yields an **individual's demand curve**. **Market demand** is then derived by taking the horizontal summation of all individual demand curves in the market.

Individual consumer behavior in microeconomics governs how changes in price, income, and related goods affect the quantity demanded of a product. [1]

## 📉 Law of Demand (Own-Price Effect)

- Relationship: Inverse between price (P) and quantity demanded ($Q_d$).
- Price rises ($\uparrow P$): Quantity demanded falls ($\downarrow Q_d$).
- Price falls ($\downarrow P$): Quantity demanded rises ($\uparrow Q_d$).
- Mechanism: Movement along the existing demand curve. [2, 3]

## 💵 Effect of Consumer Income (Y)

A change in consumer income shifts the entire demand curve. [4]

- Normal Goods:
    
    - Income rises ($\uparrow Y$): Demand rises ($\uparrow D$).
    - Income falls ($\downarrow Y$): Demand falls ($\downarrow D$). [4]
    
- Inferior Goods:
    
    - Income rises ($\uparrow Y$): Demand falls ($\downarrow D$).
    - Income falls ($\downarrow Y$): Demand rises ($\uparrow D$). [4]
    

## 🔗 Effect of Related Product Prices

Changes in prices of substitutes or complements shift the primary product's demand curve. [4]

- Substitutes (Alternative goods):
    
    - Price of substitute rises ($\uparrow P_{sub}$): Demand for main good rises ($\uparrow D$).
    - Price of substitute falls ($\downarrow P_{sub}$): Demand for main good falls ($\downarrow D$). [4]
    
- Complements (Jointly consumed goods):
    
    - Price of complement rises ($\uparrow P_{comp}$): Demand for main good falls ($\downarrow D$).
    - Price of complement falls ($\downarrow P_{comp}$): Demand for main good rises ($\uparrow D$). [4, 5]
    

## 📊 Key Elasticity Formulas

- Price Elasticity of Demand (PED):
    
    - $\text{PED} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_d}{\% \Delta P}$ [6]
    - PED = (price coefficient) * P / Q
 
- **Elastic Demand ($\text{PED} > 1$ in absolute value):** Price and total revenue move in **opposite** directions. If price goes _down_, total revenue goes _up_.
    
- **Inelastic Demand ($\text{PED} < 1$):** Price and total revenue move in the **same** direction. If price goes _down_, total revenue goes _down_.
    
- Income Elasticity of Demand (YED):
    
    - $\text{YED} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_d}{\% \ \Delta Y}$ (Positive = Normal, Negative = Inferior) [7]
    
- Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand ($E_{xy}$):
    
    - $E_{xy} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_d \text{ of } Y}{\% \Delta P \text{ of } X}$ (Positive = Substitutes, Negative = Complements) [8]
