## Summary
### I. The Efficient Market Baseline
*   **Market Efficiency:** A free market is considered efficient when the **Marginal Benefit (MB)** equals the **Marginal Cost (MC)** at the equilibrium quantity ($Q_{efficient}$). At this point, total surplus (Consumer Surplus + Producer Surplus) is maximized.
*   **Equilibrium:** The natural meeting point of demand and supply determines the quantity traded and price.

### II. Government Intervention: Efficiency vs. Equity
*   **The Trade-Off:** Government policies often involve a trade-off between **efficiency** (maximizing total surplus/economic output) and **equity** (distributing wealth or ensuring fairness).
*   **Cost of Intervention:** When the government intervenes, it typically moves the market away from $Q_{efficient}$, resulting in **deadweight loss** (a reduction in overall economic welfare).

### III. Price Controls (Quantity Restrictions)
These policies directly set a price limit and are effective only when they move *away* from the competitive equilibrium price.

1.  **Price Floor:** A government-mandated minimum price.
    *   **Effect:** Effective when set **above** equilibrium.
    *   **Result:** Creates a **quantity surplus** (Quantity Supplied > Quantity Demanded).
    *   **Example:** Minimum wage, agricultural price support.

2.  **Price Ceiling:** A government-mandated maximum price.
    *   **Effect:** Effective when set **below** equilibrium.
    *   **Result:** Creates a **quantity shortage** (Quantity Demanded > Quantity Supplied).
    *   **Example:** Rent control, gasoline caps.

### IV. Taxes and Subsidies (Behavioral Policies)
These policies alter the relative costs or benefits of transactions.

1.  **Taxes:** Reduce production/consumption and create deadweight loss.
    *   **Tax on Buyers:** Increases the effective price paid by consumers. The demand curve shifts inward.
    *   **Tax on Sellers:** Decreases the net revenue received by producers. The supply curve shifts upward.

2.  **Subsidies:** Increase production/consumption and reduce deadweight loss (though they cost the government money).
    *   **Subsidy to Buyers:** Decreases the effective price paid by consumers. The demand curve shifts outward.
    *   **Subsidy to Sellers:** Increases the net revenue received by producers. The supply curve shifts downward.

***
### 💡 Key Takeaways for Exam Prep:

*   **Always Check $Q_{efficient}$:** Before analyzing any policy, determine where MB = MC.
*   **Identify the Impact:** Know whether a policy (floor/ceiling/tax) creates a **surplus** or a **shortage**.
*   **Understand Shifts:** Remember that taxes and subsidies are not simple parallel shifts; they change the *effective* price paid by one group relative to the other.

### Taxes and Subsidies

Tax or subsidy may be imposed on the buyers or sellers - market outcome is the same

Taxes decreases production
- Price buyers pay = price sellers receive + tax
- Tax on buyers: $Q_{DT} = a - b(P + tax)$
- Tax on seller: $Q_{ST} = a + b(P - tax)$

Subsidies increases production
- Price buyers pay = price sellers receive - subsidy
- Subsidy to buyers: $Q_{DT} = a - b(P - subsidy)$
- Subsidy to sellers: $Q_{ST} = a + b(P + subsidy)$

#### Example
Q = 1700 - 250P
Q = 150P - 60

##### Tax
Tax on buyers = $Q_{DT}$ = 1700 - 250(P + tax)   <-- Where you see P, replace with (P+tax)
Tax on sellers = $Q_{ST}$ = 150(P - tax) - 60

##### Subsidy

Subsidy for buyers = $Q_{BS}$ = 1700 - 250(P - subsidy) <-- Where you see P, replace with (P+subsidy)
Subsidy for sellers = $Q_{SS}$ = 150(P + subsidy) - 60

Unit tax or subsidy: $ amount per unit
ad valorem tax or subsidy: percentage of price
